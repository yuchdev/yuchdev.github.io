Are We Chasing the Wrong Robot?

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0-MYK_i6gl8

One trend in robotics has always puzzled me: why is so much investment flowing into humanoid robots?

From an engineering perspective, the human body is an extraordinarily difficult machine to reproduce. Dynamic balance, dozens of high-precision joints, dexterous hands, limited vision, force feedback, safety around people - the list goes on. Every additional degree of freedom adds cost, complexity, maintenance, energy consumption, and failure modes.

Yet we often treat "looking like a person" as if it were a requirement.

Interestingly, another industry has already answered a similar question.

Modern military robotics rarely tries to build a real-life Terminator. Instead, it favors specialized mobile platforms with low profile and interchangeable payloads. A tracked or wheeled base can carry reconnaissance equipment, logistics cargo, explosives disposal tools, communication systems, or weapons. The platform remains the same; only the mission module changes.

Why shouldn't civilian robotics evolve in a similar direction?

Imagine home assistance. Instead of a two-legged robot awkwardly walking around your apartment and scaring the entire family, imagine a compact, low-profile autonomous platform that can roll efficiently, climb small obstacles, dock itself at a charging station, and automatically swap tools depending on the task.

Need to vacuum? Attach the cleaning module.

Need to load the dishwasher? Attach a specialized manipulator optimized for dishes.

Laundry? Swap to a different end effector.

Gardening? Different module again.

Most homes don't actually require a robot to imitate human motion - they require tasks to be completed reliably, safely, and economically.

The same philosophy extends well beyond households.

In hospitals, logistics centers, warehouses, agriculture, construction sites, and factories, purpose-built mobile platforms may deliver better performance at lower cost than forcing every robot into a humanoid form. Different environments demand different mobility systems - wheels, tracks, articulated legs, or hybrids - and different manipulators. A modular architecture allows the expensive autonomy stack to be reused while adapting the hardware to each mission.

Humanoid robots certainly have niches. Environments designed exclusively for humans, disaster response, or situations where existing tools and infrastructure cannot be modified are legitimate use cases.

But they shouldn't automatically become the default vision of robotics. We're optimizing for science-fiction aesthetics instead of engineering efficiency.

History suggests that the most successful technologies are usually not the ones that imitate nature most closely - they're the ones that solve the problem with the least complexity.

Maybe the future of robotics won't look like a family of androids living with us. Maybe it will look more like an ecosystem of specialized, modular autonomous machines quietly doing useful work without pretending to be human.

---
I don't fully understand the hype around making robots as humanoid as possible.

The argument is clear: humanoid robots can operate in environments already designed for humans - doors, stairs, shelves, vehicles. That is a real advantage.

But effective is not always efficient.

A human-like hand is the result of biological evolution: gripping, climbing, throwing, crafting, using weapons, and surviving with one general-purpose manipulator. Robots do not have to inherit that constraint.

A robot manipulator can be designed around the task. Industrial robots use specialized grippers, welding heads, cutters, surgical end-effectors, and many other task-specific tools. Emergency, research, warehouse, medical, and manufacturing robots all have different requirements.

Even better, a robot can switch the end-effector itself: drill, saw, screwdriver, gripper, sensor. Tool-changing is a simpler path to capability than imitating the full complexity of a human hand.

So I'm not saying humanoid robots are useless. For human-centered environments, they make sense. But the race to build the "most humanoid" robot often feels more like marketing than optimal engineering
