A few years ago, suggesting that AI would significantly reduce demand for at least some categories of developers was often dismissed as alarmism. Today, nobody can't deny the reality anymore: hundreds of thousands in layoffs, rising skill requirements, and downward pressure on median salaries. That's happening globally, not just in one country or sector.

What makes this different is that it's not a typical boom-and-bust cycle. Software engineering is undergoing a transformation on a scale never seen before. Nobody knows exactly where the equilibrium will settle, but pretending nothing is changing seems like a "path to become a farmer" in 2-3 years

The safest strategy today is to become difficult to replace. That means developing expertise in areas where business knowledge, architecture, performance, security, hardware, leadership, domain knowledge, or simply human hands matter more than AI. The more your value comes from judgment and responsibility rather than pure code production, the stronger your position becomes.

---

I've heard a lot of these predictions over the last two years. At this point, they feel more like a coping mechanism than an analysis.

What's becoming clear isn't that senior engineers are suddenly going to earn 10x more. It's that the market is shrinking while expectations are rising. Layoffs, higher hiring bars, fewer junior openings, and declining median salaries are no longer isolated events - they're an industry-wide trend.

The "10x engineers" who remain will increasingly supervise AI, review architecture, validate correctness, and integrate complex systems. But even that won't be a permanent safe haven. AI capabilities continue to improve, and many of today's "AI oversight" tasks will eventually be automated as well.

The question isn't whether AI will replace developers overnight - it won't. The question is where you'll still create unique value five years from now. Find that niche early, because "software engineer" is becoming too broad to be a sustainable competitive advantage.
