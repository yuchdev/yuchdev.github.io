I'm sympathetic to engineers who were laid off during this rather insane wave of "cost cutting," but this story describes a serious engineering and management failure long before the layoff happened.

Concentrating critical operational knowledge in one "god-level" developer is a textbook bus-factor risk. If a production system survives only because one person manually repairs data every night, that process should have been documented, automated, monitored, and transferred to the team.

The engineer did not have to be laid off for the same failure to surface. They could have retired, become ill, changed jobs, or simply gone on a long vacation.

So the real lesson is not "never fire the irreplaceable engineer." It is never allow an engineer to become irreplaceable in the first place. Critical systems should depend on processes and shared knowledge, not on one invisible human cron job.
