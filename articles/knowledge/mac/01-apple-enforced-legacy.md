I think you're comparing Apple's major architecture transitions, but my criticism is about something much simpler: support policy.

A MacBook Pro from 2014 is perfectly capable of running modern macOS. Yet Apple decided years ago that it shouldn't receive the latest updates.

The limitations imposed are almost always artificial rather than technical. Projects like OpenCore Legacy Patcher demonstrate that many "unsupported" Macs can run macOS 26 on decade-old hardware, it raises the question of how much of the restriction is engineering necessity versus product strategy.

That's why I view this as a consumer-rights issue rather than a technical one. If a device is technically capable of running new software, blocking it purely by policy after just a few years of support, while subsequent releases gradually become less optimized for older hardware, starts looking less like innovation and more like planned obsolescence and anti-consumer policy.

Interestingly, regulators are paying attention. The EU has been investigating right-to-repair and product-lifetime practices for years, and if deliberate shortening of product life can be demonstrated, the legal and financial consequences will follow.

I still use a MacBook Air 2017 and a MacBook Pro 2019, and I don't see a compelling reason to replace hardware that still does everything I need.

The difference is that on a typical PC (assuming no exotic hardware in use), you can install a newer Windows or Linux version. The hardware remains yours. A 10-year-old PC often remains a perfectly capable general-purpose computer.

With Apple, the lifecycle is tied to Apple's decisions. Once a machine falls off the supported list, you depend on community projects such as OpenCore Legacy Patcher to keep receiving modern MacOS releases. If the user is not tech-savvy enough to set up OCLP, 10-year-old Mac becomes useless.

This is one of the reasons why many enterprises still prefer the PC ecosystem, even privately everyone uses Apple, from CEO to developers. The value of hardware is not determined solely by its performance, UX and usability, but also by its ability to remain useful after the manufacturer has moved on.
