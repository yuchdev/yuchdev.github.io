### MODERN EDUCATIONAL CONTENT IS TRASH

I've noticed this for a long time: around 95% of "educational" content on YouTube is noise.

If you rely on AI as a tutor, it will often tell you what you want to hear, not what you actually need to master the technology. It's great at smoothing the path, but poor at enforcing discipline

The same issue exists with many modern educational platforms. They're optimized for clicks, speed, retention metrics, fast gratification, and short attention spans - which is the opposite of the focused effort that any serious programming study requires

Real learning is built on concentration and sustained effort. 
The YouTube platform, however, is optimized for fast gratification, retention tricks, and ad impressions - not for deep understanding. Most of content provided for "from total zero" level, because it's the most common search.

If you look at many popular YouTube learning channels (LUCKILY, NOT ALL), a clear pattern emerges:

- most targets absolute beginners, because that's where demand is highest
- lessons are short, both to match TikTok-era attention spans and maximize ad slots material 
- often incomplete, with quality dropping sharply toward the end, because most "zero to hero" learners never make it that far anyway

There's still 5% of high-quality content CHANNEL, where educators put their soul in - but they are gems in dunes of sand.

Content from established universities and academic institutions tends to be far more reliable than from "creators": YouTube isn't their business model. Teaching and research are.

You can call me conservative or say "ok millennial," but I still believe the most reliable way to self-study programming is well-structured courses from top universities and books (you know, with pages), complemented by sustained, hands-on practice. Everything else is secondary.

---

What we certainly do not have a shortage of is content "for absolute beginners"

That is probably 90% of educational YouTube content, and the Amazon market is already extremely saturated with beginner-friendly books, crash courses, bootcamps, and "learn X from zero" material.

But I understand why it works from a marketing perspective. "Absolute beginner" sells hope: the idea that someone can start from nothing and change their career or life direction. Many people buy that promise. Only a small percentage will actually finish the book or course, but the purchase has already happened.

In that sense, it is a much more sustainable commercial strategy than writing for experienced professionals, who usually know exactly what they need, compare alternatives critically, and are much harder to impress.

"For absolute beginners" is not just an educational category. It is also one of the strongest marketing labels in tech publishing.

---

Don't expect a quick win, and don't get discouraged if the path takes longer than you hoped. The important thing is to stay consistent and keep on track.

A young man once asked me how long it would take to go from "starting Python" to "getting hired." I estimated roughly 9-12 months of consistent fulltime work on yourself: enough time to learn the language properly, choose and study an applied direction - backend, ML, DevOps, security - build portfolio projects, apply, get rejected, identify gaps, improve, and iterate multiple times.

He was terrified: "But I expected to do it in three months!"

As if my approval could bend reality for him.

The problem is that many beginners expect immediate payback. They may even work hard, but when hard work does not quickly turn into a job offer, they assume something is wrong and give up.

In tech, consistency matters more than initial enthusiasm. Three months can give you a start. Nine to twelve months can give you a foundation. Several years can give you a career.

### C++ AND MODERN EDUCATIONAL CONTENT

For a while, I've been actively looking for a platform where I could teach Advanced C++ on my own terms - with my own curriculum, pacing, and standards.
After talking to multiple education providers, I came to a simple conclusion:
running a fully independent course (most likely via Udemy or a similar marketplace) is the only realistic option.

Almost every platform I spoke to tried to interfere in some way:
"Can we simplify the material?"
"Can we split it into shorter lessons?"
"Can we reshape the structure to improve engagement?"

I already took for a fact that modern educational platforms are optimized optimized for clicks, speed, and retention metrics, fast gratification and short attention spans, which is opposite to the long, focused effort, that any minimally serious study of any technology actually requires.

And Advanced C++ is the worst possible fit for dopamine-optimized learning.
You don't really learn systems programming in:
- 10-minute videos
- "Zero to hero" playlists
- perfectly smooth, frictionless tutorials

Real understanding comes from wrestling with abstractions, hitting compiler errors you don't immediately understand, revisiting the same concept weeks and months later and finally getting it.

If you look at many popular YouTube learning channels (thankfully, not all of them), you'll see the same pattern:
- most of content designed for absolute beginners, because that's what people usually look for
- short lessons, because this is what TikTok audience accustomed to, and also because you can stick more ads in the content 
- incomplete material, with quality sharply decreasing by the end, because most of "Zero to hero" listeners will likely drop it on the way

I myself witnessed such "graduate" of "complete" C++ course with total length of 4 hours, inquiring if he can enter the industry now.

This leads to very real, very visible outcomes.

Historical/modern examples:
Tutorial hell - people consume endless content but can't build basic application besides copied mindlessly from the screen, or reason independently.
3-months-bootcamp graduates genuinely upset their money were lost, because industry requires at least 9-12 months of fulltime ramp-up before becoming productive at least as a Junior Engineer - because the industry expects depth, not familiarity.

Learning is not supposed to feel easy. It requires time, struggle, and feedback - not shortcuts. But the same time, it can be deeply rewarding 

There's a unique kind of satisfaction that comes only after months, not days of real effort. After watching hundreds of hours of material. After reading actual books - you know, the ones with pages and margins. After writing code that breaks, gets fixed, breaks again, and finally holds.
The kind that stays with you, compounds over years, and quietly separates professionals constantly striving for knowledge from content consumers.
